# SkillDelta: When Does a Skill Add Value? Task-Conditional Gain Prediction for Selective Skill Use

- arXiv: 2609.32274v1, 26 Sep 2026, cs.AI. License CC BY 4.0.
- Authors: Anjie Xu, Zhiyu Zhang, Ruiqing Ding, Fengli Xu, Leye Wang (Peking University, Zhongguancun Academy, Hefei University of Technology, Tsinghua).
- Read on 2026-11-20 from the arXiv HTML full text plus the PDF (22 pages).
- Code: public repository with predictor, evaluation, data collection and a DeepSeek Harness plugin. Task-level outcomes and embeddings are in a review supplement, not in the public release.

## Claim (key number and where it is)

You can predict, before the agent acts, whether a supplied skill will raise its chance of success, by looking at paired with-skill and without-skill runs on similar past tasks.
Key numbers:
- Paired history ranks observed gains better than with-skill outcomes alone in 12 of 15 settings (Figure 2, Section 4.3.1).
- At the same expected skill-use rate, SkillDelta beats random activation in all 15 settings, by 4.33 points of success on average (Figure 6, Section 4.3.2).
- That 4.33 splits into 3.72 [3.47, 3.91] from choosing which task GROUPS get the skill and only 0.61 [0.37, 0.93] from choosing tasks inside a group (Figure 7, Appendix B.3).
The honest summary in the paper itself: the value is mostly group-level allocation. Task-level selection is clear only on ToolQA.

## Method (enough to reproduce)

Definitions (Section 2):
- Gain tau(t, s) = P(success with skill s) minus P(success with no skill), same agent, same protocol.
- Observed paired gain D_i = mean success with skill minus mean success without, over r_i repetitions. With one pair D is in {-1, 0, +1}: harm, no change, help.
- Policy: use the skill if predicted gain > eta. Default eta = 0.

Predictor (Section 3.3, Algorithm 1):
1. Keep a bank of past tasks with: task text, skill or family id, a fixed question embedding, and paired outcomes.
2. For a new task, restrict the bank to tasks in the same family (same supplied skill, or the recorded task group).
3. Embed the question only with text-embedding-3-small (1,536-d, L2-normalized). The skill text is not embedded.
4. Take up to k = 6 nearest neighbours by cosine.
5. Predicted gain = weighted mean of the neighbours' observed gains, weights = cosine clipped at zero, normalized. If all weights are zero use uniform weights. If there is no support, return 0 and skip the skill.
6. Use the skill if the prediction is strictly positive.
No model is trained. No LLM call is made at decision time.

Theory (Section 3.2, Appendix A):
- Assumption: |tau(t) - tau(t')| <= L d(phi(t), phi(t')) + eps_phi. Nearby tasks have similar gains.
- Theorem 1: error <= L rho_w (coverage) + eps_phi (representation) + sqrt(2 log(2/delta) sum w_i^2 / r_i) (execution noise).
- Proposition 3: decision regret is at most the gain error.
- Proposition 4: without a transfer assumption, no amount of history identifies the sign of gain on a new task.
- Corollary 7: with n support tasks, k neighbours, r repeats, the rate is of order (nr)^(-1/(nu+2)).
- The authors note the bound is vacuous at their main setting: at delta = 0.05, k = 6 the noise term is at least 1.109 for r = 1 and 0.640 for r = 3, while |tau| <= 1 (Appendix A.3).

Evaluation protocol (Section 4.2):
- Leave-one-task-out inside each benchmark inventory. Both outcomes of the target are removed from its support.
- Policy outcomes are reconstructed from the recorded runs: take the with-skill record if the action is use, else the no-skill record. No new runs.
- Baselines: Always-off, Always-on, random activation at matched use rate, direct self-judgment (the agent sees the question and the skill text and answers use or no-use), a TF-IDF task-skill relevance gate with a median threshold, a with-skill-only predictor (same neighbours, averages with-skill success instead of gains), TF-IDF kNN, family mean, single and dual Ridge.

## Setting, benchmarks, models

- Benchmarks and inventory (Table 1): ToolQA 1,430 tasks, 14 skills. MedCalc-Bench 1,100 tasks, 55 skills. BigCodeBench 1,136 tasks, 139 skills. LogicBench 760 tasks, 19 skills. SpreadsheetBench 399 tasks, 1 skill. The first four come from SRA-Bench. Total 4,825 evaluable tasks.
- Agents ("target-agent stacks"): Qwen-Turbo, GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek-V4-Flash. All are the model that uses the skill. Temperature zero. Outcomes are never pooled across agents.
- Skills: the supplied SRA-Bench skill for ToolQA and LogicBench, a calculator skill for MedCalc-Bench, all gold skills injected together for BigCodeBench (575 distinct bundles, 2 to 6 skills per task), and one externally optimized spreadsheet skill.
- Who wrote the skills? The paper does not name an author model. Skills are benchmark-supplied or external artifacts. They are not written by the agent that uses them, and they are not written for a specific failure. There is no teacher model anywhere in the paper.
- Repetitions: Qwen and GLM use one paired run per task (three repeats on LogicBench). DeepSeek uses three-repeat means on all five. Record counts: 12,690 condition records each for Qwen and GLM, 28,950 for DeepSeek (Appendix B.1).

## Metrics (exact)

- Gain-sign AUROC: ranks tasks by predicted gain against the label "observed gain > 0" versus "observed gain <= 0" on held-out tasks.
- Task success (%) of the resulting policy, reconstructed from recorded runs.
- Token savings (%) relative to Always-on. Execution tokens only. Excludes bank construction, encoding and retrieval. Self-judge includes its routing call.
- Matched-rate advantage: success of the policy minus expected success of random activation at the same use rate, = (1/N) sum (a_i - p) D_i (Equation 21).
- Balanced accuracy for use or skip decisions (Table 3, Table 7).
- Gain MAE against three-repeat targets (Figure 4).

## Results (including weak and negative ones)

Prediction quality:
- AUROC on Qwen-Turbo (Table 4): ToolQA 0.879, MedCalc 0.781, BigCodeBench 0.527, LogicBench 0.621, SpreadsheetBench 0.580.
- AUROC 95% intervals for all 15 panels (Table 5). Qwen: [0.849, 0.907], [0.751, 0.810], [0.478, 0.577], [0.574, 0.667], [0.478, 0.681]. GLM: [0.792, 0.875], [0.819, 0.891], [0.520, 0.616], [0.550, 0.634], [0.638, 0.745]. DeepSeek: [0.751, 0.811], [0.793, 0.852], [0.526, 0.604], [0.599, 0.693], [0.607, 0.721]. Order: ToolQA, MedCalc, BigCode, Logic, Spreadsheet.
- So it is good on ToolQA and MedCalc (about 0.78 to 0.88), weak on LogicBench and SpreadsheetBench (about 0.58 to 0.69), and near chance on BigCodeBench (0.53 to 0.57).
- Paired evidence beats the outcome-free reference on 12 panels, beats with-skill-only on 12, beats both on 10. On BigCodeBench the outcome-free lexical score ranks better than the paired predictor on all three agents. With-skill-only beats paired on Qwen BigCodeBench, Qwen SpreadsheetBench and DeepSeek ToolQA.
- Representation matters: TF-IDF kNN beats the embedding on 3 of 5 Qwen benchmarks. BigCodeBench 0.527 to 0.582 (Table 4, Section 6).

Self-judgment (the agent's own forecast):
- The agent says "use" on more than 91% of tasks in 14 of 15 panels. The exception is DeepSeek SpreadsheetBench at 44.4% (Section 4.3.1, Table 3).
- SkillDelta's AUROC is above self-judge on all 15 panels.
- Balanced-accuracy advantage of SkillDelta over self-judge: from -0.03 [-3.36, 3.21] (DeepSeek BigCodeBench) to +30.41 [27.99, 32.75] (Qwen ToolQA). Intervals include zero on three panels (Table 3).
- Self-judge costs tokens: 1,164 to 5,514 router tokens per task. Total tokens rise 2.6 to 98.2% over Always-on.

Decision value (Table 2):
- SkillDelta beats Always-off on success and saves tokens against Always-on in all 15 panels. 14 of 15 success-gain intervals exclude zero. The exception is Qwen SpreadsheetBench [-0.8, 5.5].
- But mean success is 63.12% against 64.04% for Always-on. SkillDelta is below Always-on in 12 of 15 panels. It is above on GLM ToolQA (47.8 vs 45.0), GLM MedCalc (88.3 vs 83.3) and DeepSeek MedCalc (90.1 vs 89.4).
- Mean token saving 20.8%, range 3.0 to 49.4%.
- Largest losses against Always-on: Qwen BigCodeBench 47.4 vs 51.4, GLM BigCodeBench 59.8 vs 63.3, DeepSeek BigCodeBench 57.7 vs 61.5.
- Within-group selection is positive in only 5 of 15 panels. ToolQA mean 4.41, all three intervals exclude zero. BigCodeBench is -1.67 to -0.25, and the Qwen interval is entirely negative. All other within-group intervals include zero (Appendix B.3).
- k was chosen after the fact. k = 3 gives 61.83% success, 4.63 matched-rate advantage, 26.87% token savings. k = 6 gives 63.12, 4.33, 20.83 (Appendix B.2).
- Ridge controls (Table 6): a learned policy constrained to match Always-on on validation saves 0 to 62% tokens. On test, accuracy difference to Always-on runs from -8.64 [-14.55, -2.70] (GLM MedCalc) to +1.75.

Prospective panels, Qwen only (Table 7, Appendix D). Predictions were frozen before target runs:
- ToolQA, unseen tasks in 8 families: 96 known, 160 target, AUROC 0.753, balanced accuracy 0.689, success +20.00 over Always-off, 3.62% tokens saved.
- MedCalc, new tasks over 55 families: 440 known, 660 target, AUROC 0.719, BA 0.667, +16.21, 32.35%.
- BigCode, scale follow-up: 200 known, 150 target, AUROC 0.716, BA 0.663, +2.00, 64.43%.
- BigCode, natural remaining distribution: 350 known, 200 target, AUROC 0.502, BA 0.572, +7.00, 32.88%. Ranking falls to chance under shift.
- These panels use different k and thresholds from the main analysis. None tests unseen skills.

Help and harm counts (Appendix C): on ToolQA, Qwen has 265 helpful and 35 harmful pairs out of 1,430. GLM has 177 helpful and 86 harmful. The rest are ties. So about 80% of single pairs show no change.

Skill effect by agent (my reading of Table 2, the authors do not analyse it): Always-on minus Always-off on MedCalc is +28.5 for Qwen (43.2 to 71.7), +4.9 for GLM (78.4 to 83.3), +15.1 for DeepSeek (74.3 to 89.4). The agent that is weakest without the skill gains most there. The pattern is not consistent across benchmarks.

## Limitations

Authors' (Section 6, Appendices B, C, D):
- Embedding similarity need not track similarity in skill benefit. TF-IDF sometimes does better.
- Sparse groups and mixed skill bundles limit BigCodeBench (86 focal groups, median size 3, 29 singletons).
- Token savings exclude building the bank. "Collecting a bank from scratch adds an investment whose value depends on reuse." Agent or skill changes require rechecking.
- k = 6 was harmonized after trajectories were collected.
- Intervals hold scores and actions fixed. They leave out refitting and configuration choice.
- The theoretical bound cannot certify decisions at k = 6, r = 1.
- No panel tests unseen skills.
- Single-pair labels are noisy. Two independent 50% conditions give D = +1 a quarter of the time with zero true gain.

Mine:
- The bank needs BOTH arms run on many similar tasks first. For a brand-new failure there is nothing to look up. This is a warm-start method.
- The main evaluation is a retrospective replay, not a live policy. Only four Qwen panels are prospective.
- The headline "beats random at matched rate" hides that the policy loses to Always-on in 12 of 15 panels. The gain is token saving, not success.
- Most value is "which skill family is worth it for this agent". A per-family average would capture most of it. Table 4 shows family mean has higher success on all five Qwen benchmarks, at the price of fewer tokens saved.
- Cost is counted in tokens only. No dollars, no latency, no build cost, no break-even.
- Only one form of help (an injected text skill) and only a binary choice.
- Skill provenance and quality are not variables. We do not know if a better-written skill would change the picture.
- Qwen and GLM main results rest on one pair per task, so many "harm" labels may be noise.

## Assumptions not tested

- Smoothness of gain in question-embedding space (Equation 4). L and eps_phi are never estimated.
- Independence across tasks and repetitions at temperature zero on hosted APIs.
- Stability of the agent and skill over time (hosted models can change).
- That a family label is known for every new task at decision time.
- That gains transfer to skills never seen in the bank.
- That reconstructed policy outcomes equal what a live gated run would produce.

## Mapping to Loopy components

- C1 (forms compared, same failure, same teacher, same student): NOT. One form only, a supplied text skill, use or skip. No program form, no teacher.
- C2 (student strength): NOT as an analysis, PARTLY as data. Three agents are run and reported separately (Table 2). The authors never relate skill gain to agent strength.
- C3 (predict before paying): COVERED for one narrow case: binary use or skip of an existing skill, predicted from paired history of similar tasks (Section 3.3, Figure 2, Tables 4, 5, 7). The agent's own judgment is tested and is uninformative (Section 4.3.1, Table 3). NOT covered: teacher diagnosis, trace features, a first-time failure with no history, a choice among different remedies.
- C4 (economics over repeats): PARTLY. Execution-token savings against Always-on (Table 2, Figure 3). A cost head in dual Ridge (Table 6). Bank cost is excluded and no break-even is computed (Section 6, Deployment cost). Unit is tokens only.
- C5 (online per-task choice among rent, coach, compile): PARTLY. A per-task gate before the first model step, with a plugin (Section 3.3). But two arms only (skill or no skill), no cloud arm, no build decision, and no online updating is evaluated.
- C6 (harm): COVERED. Harm is part of the definition (D = -1) and is counted (ToolQA: 35 harmful of 1,430 for Qwen, 86 for GLM, Appendix C). Negative within-group selection on BigCodeBench (Appendix B.3). Abstract: skills "often provide no benefit, and can even hurt performance while incurring additional token costs".

## What this means for Loopy

What we can no longer claim:
- That we are first to show a skill can hurt an agent, or that most of the time it changes nothing. SkillDelta counts it.
- That we are first to predict before execution whether help will help. SkillDelta does it for use or skip.
- That "the small model's own forecast is uninformative" is a new finding. SkillDelta shows self-judgment says "use" over 91% of the time in 14 of 15 panels and loses to a history-based score on all 15. It also cites SRA-Bench for the same point. Our forecast is of failure, theirs is of benefit, so cite them and state the difference.
- That the right target is success with help. They show the right target is the paired difference.

What stays open:
- Predicting WHICH remedy works (note versus function versus cloud), not only whether one fixed text skill helps.
- Prediction at the first failure, with no paired history. SkillDelta needs a bank of both arms.
- Using the teacher's diagnosis or features of the failed trace as predictors. They use only the question embedding and a family label.
- Build economics: cost of making the help, failed builds, break-even over repeats, in dollars or cloud calls.
- Whether an executable form removes the harm cases that text skills show.

Design lessons to copy:
- Always run the paired arm (with and without the remedy) on the same task. Report the gain, not the treated success.
- Compare any selector against random selection at the SAME use rate. Then split the advantage into between-group and within-group (Equation 21). It will tell us if our predictor only learns "task type".
- Repeat runs. One pair per task gives noisy labels. Their MAE falls with every extra repetition in all nine panels.
- Leave-one-task-out, bootstrap intervals with decisions fixed, and say what the intervals leave out.
- Include a "with-help-only" predictor and a self-judge baseline as controls.
- Freeze predictions before running targets for any prospective claim.
- Be honest about n. Their own bound is vacuous at k = 6 with one repetition. Our 9-task pilot cannot support any prediction claim, only motivate one.
