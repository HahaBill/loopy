# 2610.09944 — AgentTime: Can Agents Estimate and Control Their Own Runtime?

Michael Ofengenden (MATS), Maksym Andriushchenko (ELLIS Tübingen / MPI-IS). arXiv:2610.09944v2 [cs.AI], 8 Oct 2026. Preprint, 30 pages.
Code: github.com/michaelofengenden/agenttimebench. Run data: agenttimebench.com.
Source read: `lit_fulltext/2610.09944.txt` (full, references included) and PDF pages 1-9 and 16-30 for every figure and table.

## Claim

A benchmark of 222 tasks from 18 existing benchmarks tests three skills of agents in their native CLI harnesses: follow a requested working duration, forecast own runtime, and estimate elapsed time afterwards.

Key numbers:

- Duration-following (Table 3, Figure 1, 1,991 runs). "On time" means runtime is 0.95 to 1.05 times the request. GPT-6 Astra (Codex) 63% on time, deviation 1.18x. GPT-5.6 Sol (Codex) 39%, 1.77x. Fable 5.1 (Claude Code) 4%, 2.86x.
- Forecasting (Section 4.2, Figure 3, 222 tasks per agent). Typical miss factor: Fable 5.1 2.6x, Astra 2.9x, Sol 3.8x. Most forecasts are too high: 63%, 66%, 83%.
- Retrospection (Section 4.3, Figure 4, 23 tasks). With time cues the estimates are near exact (1.03x to 1.33x). With time cues scrubbed: Fable 2.58x, Astra 2.62x, Sol 5.38x.
- On time is not working: of 158 classifiable Astra runs, 14 explicitly slept (9%) and 74 re-checked earlier work (47%) (Section 4.1, Figure 2b).

## Method (enough to reuse)

Suite (Table 2). 18 benchmarks: GPQA Diamond 28 tasks, HLE 24, AppWorld 18 (requests 1.5 to 40 min), AssistantBench 18, OSWorld 2.0 16, Terminal-Bench 4.0 16, ProgramBench 14, Agents' Last Exam 12, CORE-Bench v1.1 12, DeepSWE v1.1 12, TUA-Bench 12, PPTArena 10, ALE-Bench 10, WildClawBench 10, PaperBench 3, YC-Bench 3, METR public tasks 2, PostTrainBench v1.1 2.

Environment. Fresh Docker container, fresh session and workspace for every run. Native time caps and budgets removed. A supervisor outside the container runs a monotonic timer from task delivery to session end. The benchmark's own grader scores the result. Agents can call a clock.

Agents. Main: Fable 5.1 in Claude Code, GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-6 Astra in Codex. All at maximum reasoning effort. Preliminary studies only: Opus 4.8, Opus 5, Fable 5, GPT-5.5.

Three experiments:

1. Duration-following. Append "Please work on this task for a full {N} {unit}. Your goal over that period is to complete the task to the best of your ability." Three requests per task, rising in steps of four (for example 1.25, 5, 20 min). One run per task, agent and request. Hidden cutoff at twice the largest request (41 runs hit it). Metrics: on time, early, late, deviation (geometric mean of the miss factor), log-log slope of runtime on request, pooled and within task.
2. Forecasting. Separate session, same harness: "How long will it take you to complete this task: "{task}" return minutes = <Number of minutes>". Compared with the natural runtime of a separate run with no requested duration. Deviation is the geometric mean of max(forecast/runtime, runtime/forecast). Also a "compression exponent" beta from `log(forecast) = a + beta·log(runtime)`. Beta = 1 means forecasts scale with runtime. Beta = 0 means a flat prior.
3. Retrospection. After a finished natural run, ask "How long did it take you to complete this task?". Five conditions remove time evidence in stages (Appendix D table): Oracle (session fork, tools plus clock), Native (fork, ordinary tools), Context-only (fork, tools off), Replay (rebuilt transcript through the API, no tools), Scrubbed (rebuilt transcript with timestamps and time cues removed, no tools). Two answers per condition.

## Results, including weak and negative ones

Duration-following:

- Within-task slopes: Fable 0.24, Sol 0.58, Astra 0.90. Asked for 16 times more time, Fable runs 1.9 times longer, Sol 5.0, Astra 12.1.
- Fable runs long on the shortest request 79% of the time (median 1.8x) and stops early on the longest 89% of the time (median 0.3x). It mostly takes as long as the task needs. Exception: open-ended benchmarks (slope 0.77 against 0.16 elsewhere).
- Harness swap (Table 3 lower block, Appendix B, 98 matched runs). The gap follows the model. Model accounts for about 0.7 of the slope gap, harness for about 0.15.
- More time rarely helps. Longest request is typically 16x the shortest. Score was the same for 62% to 64% of tasks and higher for 23% to 25% (Figure 2c).

Forecasting (the part that matters most for Loopy):

- Median forecast is 15 minutes for all three agents. Median natural runtimes: Fable 13 min, Astra 10, Sol 6.
- Compression exponent: Fable 0.89 [0.78, 0.99], Sol 0.89 [0.78, 1.01], Astra 1.12 [0.97, 1.29]. So in the main suite forecasts do scale with runtime. The error is mostly a steady overestimate: geometric mean forecast/runtime about 3 for Sol, 1.3 for Fable, 1.4 for Astra.
- This is the reverse of the human planning fallacy.
- Following and forecasting dissociate. Astra follows best (1.18x) but forecasts worse (2.9x) than Fable (2.6x), which follows worst.
- Fable 5.1 gave no number for 6 of 222 tasks. Two of its 216 numbers were zero (Appendix C).
- No per-benchmark forecast accuracy is reported. So there is no AppWorld-specific forecast number.

Preliminary studies (Appendix E, ProgramBench, older models, not pooled with the main results):

- E.1 same-turn forecast, 200 runs each. Opus 4.8: mean forecast 99 min against 85 actual, 1.16x, 64% within 1.5x, exponent 0.24, r = 0.38. GPT-5.5: 72 against 17.5, 4.12x, 1% within 1.5x, exponent 0.19, r = 0.15. Both forecast about 90 minutes for almost every task. Opus looks calibrated only because its runtimes happen to average near its flat guess.
- E.2 separate-turn forecast. Opus 5 median runtime 93 min, Sol 29 min. More evidence before the forecast (task text, plus docs, plus a sandbox) does not reduce error. For Sol it makes forecasts longer.
- E.3 referent wording, 4,100 sessions. Geometric-mean forecast for "you": Fable 5 7.5 min, Sol 13.5. For "a frontier AI agent": 9.9, 15.6. For "a skilled human professional": 32.1, 35.8. Swapping "complete" for "finish" moves forecasts by about 10%.
- E.5, 200 runs per harness. Correlation with true runtime (Pearson on logs): before-run estimate 0.41 (Claude Code) and 0.17 (Codex). After-run report 0.86 and 0.48. Visible event count 0.91 and 0.52. A plain transcript-length count matches or beats the agent's own report.
- E.6 self-score. Asked for a 0 to 100 self-score before seeing tests. Opus 4.8: +20 points overconfident, r = 0.28, n = 178. GPT-5.5: +21 points, r = 0.29, n = 198. Both sit near 80% whatever the true score. Separate-turn: Sol overestimates (mid-90s for runs below 50%). Opus 5 underestimates by more than 10 points. On php-src both guessed about 70% when the true scores were 7% and 14.5%.

Negative or weak:

- 28 of Astra's 44 agentic runs in Claude Code failed a campaign check (12 for using subagents). Times kept.
- Grading is incomplete: 1,911 of 1,991 runs have a native score. 56 more score 0 because nothing was left to grade.
- Many grades were collected after the campaign, some from disk snapshots. PaperBench was re-judged with a different judge model.
- Retrospective scrubbed deviation is driven by GPQA runs. Without them: Fable 2.0x, Sol 1.7x, Astra 1.4x.

## Direct answers to our questions

- What do agents estimate? Their own wall-clock runtime before a task (forecast) and after it (retrospection). In a preliminary study also their own score (0 to 100). They never estimate their chance of failure before the task.
- How is accuracy measured? Geometric mean of the miss factor, share too high, and the log-log slope. By model: forecast 2.6x (Fable 5.1), 2.9x (Astra), 3.8x (Sol). Retrospect with cues 1.03x to 1.33x. Scrubbed 2.58x, 2.62x, 5.38x.
- Does accuracy depend on model size? Not tested. All agents are frontier models at maximum effort. There is no small model. Section 5.1 only conjectures that control will improve with larger models, citing other work. The older-model numbers come from a different task set, so the generation comparison is confounded.
- Is the estimate used to make any decision? No. The forecast is elicited in a separate session and only compared with the measured runtime. Nothing is routed, budgeted or escalated from it.

## Limitations

Authors' (Section 6):

- One run per task and request. Run-to-run variance is not measured, apart from 55 question runs repeated through another provider.
- Three agents from two labs.
- Runtime depends on serving speed. Runs went through subscriptions, API keys and OpenRouter.
- One instruction wording, maximum reasoning effort only.
- Requests beyond 8.3 hours come from four tasks.
- Cost: about $93,000 in total.

Mine:

- Forecasts are point estimates in minutes. No probabilities, so no calibration curve.
- The forecast is of runtime. It is not a forecast of success or failure. Only the preliminary self-score touches success, and that is asked after the work.
- No small or cheap model. Nothing here speaks to a weak agent's self-knowledge.
- No per-benchmark forecast error, so nothing specific to AppWorld beyond the pooled score.
- Natural runtime comes from a single run, so part of the "forecast error" is run-to-run noise.
- Preliminary studies use other models and one benchmark. The flat-prior finding (exponent 0.19 to 0.24) and the main-suite finding (0.89 to 1.12) cannot be compared cleanly.
- Model names and harness versions are tied to one moment.

## Assumptions not tested

- That one natural run is a fair estimate of "how long the task takes this agent".
- That forecast quality would be the same at lower reasoning effort or in smaller models.
- That the minutes format does not itself push answers to round numbers (median forecast is 15 for all three agents).
- That better forecasts would improve any downstream decision. No decision is tested.

## Mapping to Loopy components

- C3 (self-predictions and their accuracy): covered for runtime (Section 4.2, Figure 3, Appendix E.1 to E.5). Partly for success (Appendix E.6 self-score, after the fact, older models). Not covered for a prior probability of failure. Not covered for small models.
- C4 (economics over repeats): not covered. Tasks do not repeat. No buy cost, break-even or guarantee. Only the campaign's own compute bill.
- C5 (online rule over options): not covered. No rule uses an estimate. Related work points to BAGEN (Lin et al. 2026, arXiv 2606.00198, agents estimating remaining resource need and warning) and Calibrate-Then-Act (Ding et al. 2026, arXiv 2602.16699, cost-aware exploration) as the decision-side papers.

## What this means for Loopy

- It backs our pilot finding in kind, with frontier models. Self-estimates lean on a prior. In the preliminary study both agents said about 90 minutes for almost everything, and self-scores sat near 80% with r about 0.28. Our small model answering 0% or 95% is the same failure in a cruder form.
- But it is not a blanket "self-forecasts are useless". In the main suite, frontier forecasts scale with runtime (slope 0.89 to 1.12) and miss by 2.6x to 3.8x, mostly by a constant overestimate. A bias like that can be corrected. A flat prior cannot. Our write-up should say which of the two our small model shows.
- Nobody here tested a weak model, a failure forecast, or AppWorld-level forecast accuracy. Our pilot result on a small model's failure forecast is not duplicated by this paper.
- Cheap observable signals beat asking the agent. Visible event count tracks runtime at r = 0.91 against 0.41 for the agent's own before-run estimate. For Loopy: predict "will the small model fail on this family" from traces and past outcomes, not from its self-report.
- More context before the forecast did not help (Figure 9), and wording moved answers by 10% to 4x (Figure 10). So prompt tuning of the small model's forecast is a weak lever.
- No paper in this line uses the self-estimate inside a rent/coach/compile rule. C5 with an uninformative forecast stays open.
- Citable caveat for our own evaluation: one run per cell hides variance. We should repeat cells.
- AppWorld is in their suite (18 tasks). Frontier agents score 61% to 76% there, pooled over durations. That is a rough reference for the "cloud" side, under a different harness and prompt.
